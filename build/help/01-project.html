The research question

The background you need, and the question you will answer. Come back to this page whenever you lose track of why a step matters.

1 · The circadian clock

Nearly every cell in the body has its own circadian clock. At its centre are two proteins, CLOCK and BMAL1, which pair up and switch other genes on. They do this by binding a short DNA sequence near the start of a gene, called an E-box. The strongest circadian E-box is the six-base palindrome CACGTG. Genes with an E-box in their promoter can be turned up and down over the 24-hour day, and a large fraction of the genome is controlled in this way ().

Promoter, TSS and E-box
  • A gene is transcribed (copied into RNA) starting at its transcription start site (TSS).
  • The promoter is the stretch of DNA around the TSS that holds most of the sequences controlling the gene. In this project we use the region from 1000 bases before each TSS to 500 bases after it.
  • An E-box is one of those control sequences: CACGTG, the site CLOCK:BMAL1 binds. A variant that changes any of its six bases may stop it working.

2 · Chronomic medicine

Our group works on what happens where the clock meets rare genetic disease, which we call chronomic medicine. A variant that changes when a disease gene is switched on, rather than the protein it makes, could make the disease milder or more severe. It could also be missed altogether, because it lies outside the protein-coding part of the gene. We have already found that variants in E-boxes of the heart gene SCN5A may matter in the arrhythmia disorder Brugada syndrome (). This project asks the same kind of question about genes that matter in the brain.

3 · Why epilepsy and brain channelopathy genes?

Seizures are closely linked to sleep and the clock: many epilepsies are worse at particular times of day or after a night of poor sleep (). Many of the genes behind these conditions encode ion channels and transporters, and the diseases they cause are known as channelopathies. If the clock helps to control when these genes are active, their promoters should contain E-boxes, and the bases in those E-boxes should be protected from change. That is what you will test.

You will work with two gene lists, both from Genomics England PanelApp ():

  • Brain channelopathy: 21 well-established ("green") genes, including SCN1A, SCN8A, CACNA1A, KCNQ2 and SLC2A1. These are the genes you will look at most closely.
  • Early-onset or syndromic epilepsy: 632 green genes. This larger set gives you enough data for statistical tests.

4 · Hypothesis and questions

Hypothesis

If timing by CLOCK:BMAL1 matters for these genes, the E-boxes in their promoters should (a) occur more often than expected by chance, (b) be unusually well conserved across mammals, and (c) carry fewer, and rarer, human variants than comparable positions. The last two together would be the signature of purifying selection, which removes changes that damage the site.

In practice, you will answer four questions:

  1. Where are the E-boxes in these promoters, and are there more than you would expect by chance?
  2. Which human variants (from the 1000 Genomes Project) destroy or create an E-box, and how common are they in different populations?
  3. Are E-box positions more conserved, and less variable, than matched control positions, as selection would predict?
  4. Which variants are the best candidates to follow up in the epilepsy patients of the 100,000 Genomes Project?

5 · What you will produce

  • A catalogue of the variants that change E-boxes in these promoters, with their frequencies in different populations.
  • A figure and a statistical test for conservation and selection.
  • A ranked shortlist of candidate variants, and a short written report that would let someone else reproduce your results.

Each of these is a result whichever way the numbers come out. If you find no signal, showing that clearly is still a finding.

Public data only

You will only use public reference data (the 1000 Genomes Project), never patient data. Any analysis of 100,000 Genomes Project patients would come later, inside the secure Genomics England Research Environment, and is not part of this guide.
