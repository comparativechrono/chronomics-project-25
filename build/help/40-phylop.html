Conservation: has evolution kept this letter still?

If an E-box really is an important switch, breaking it should have been bad for our ancestors too — so evolution should have kept those letters unchanged across species. A conservation score measures exactly that.

What phyloP is

phyloP compares the same position across many species' genomes and asks whether it has changed less (conserved — positive score) or more (fast-evolving — negative score) than neutral DNA (). We use the 470-way mammalian alignment from the Zoonomia project, the state of the art for spotting constrained regulatory DNA (). A score above ~2 means a position has been held unusually still across mammals.

We saved phyloP for every base of every promoter so you don't have to download the huge genome-wide file:

zcat /data/examples/conservation/phylop_promoters.csv.gz | head -3

Read conservation at an E-box

Load the table and look at the scores under one of your E-boxes. Pick a canonical E-box from your list and read the six positions:

import pandas as pd
phylop = pd.read_csv("/data/examples/conservation/phylop_promoters.csv.gz")
phylop = phylop.set_index(["chrom","pos"])

chrom, s, e, gene = eboxes[0]          # first E-box (BED: 0-based s)
for p in range(s+1, e+1):
    print(p, gene, phylop.loc[(chrom, p), "phyloP470"])

Are those six numbers high (conserved) or low? Try a few E-boxes, then a few random promoter positions, and get a feel for the difference before you measure it properly in the next step.

Why conservation adds something new

The PWM score (Part 3) is a prediction from a laboratory matrix. phyloP is independent evidence from evolution: it knows nothing about CLOCK:BMAL1, only about what has survived millions of years. When a predicted switch is also deeply conserved, the case that it is real gets much stronger.
