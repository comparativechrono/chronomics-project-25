Where you could take it

If the main project goes well and you want to do more, here are some possible extensions. Each one uses skills you already have.

  • Tandem E-boxes. The strongest circadian control sites are often two E-boxes about 6–7 bases apart. Search your E-box list for pairs with that spacing. Are the tandem ones even more conserved?
  • Does site strength predict constraint? Plot PWM score against phyloP for every E-box. If the highest-scoring sites are also the most conserved, that is good evidence that they are functional.
  • Other non-canonical E-boxes. You concentrated on CACGTG and the PWM. Try the related sequences CACGTT and CATGTG. Are they enriched, or conserved?
  • Timing of expression. Compare your genes with a database of rhythmically expressed human genes (Ruben et al.). Do the genes with conserved E-boxes tend to be the rhythmic ones?
  • A different gene set. The whole analysis starts from the gene list, so you can swap in a cardiac panel (closer to the group's work on SCN5A) or a neurodevelopmental one and compare.
  • gnomAD. Look up your top variants in gnomAD v4 (over 800,000 people, about 76,000 of them with whole genomes) for much more precise frequencies and any sign of constraint.
The general method

All of these extensions follow the same pattern: define regions, find a motif, overlay variation and test against a control. That pattern covers a large part of regulatory genomics. The question here, about the clock in brain genes, is new, but the method works for many others.
