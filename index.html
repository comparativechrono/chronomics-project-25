The research question

The background you need, and the exact question you will answer. Read this once; come back to it whenever you want to remember why a step matters.

1 · Almost every cell keeps time

Humans have a circadian clock in nearly every cell. At its core, two proteins — CLOCK and BMAL1 — join together and switch other genes on. They do this by sticking to a short, specific run of DNA letters in front of a gene, called an E-box. The strongest circadian E-box is the six-letter palindrome CACGTG. Genes with an E-box in their promoter can be turned up and down across the 24-hour day; a large fraction of the genome is timed this way (Takahashi 2017; Partch et al. 2014; Koike et al. 2012; Mohawk et al. 2012).

Promoter, TSS and E-box — the words you need
  • A gene is transcribed (copied into RNA) starting at its transcription start site (TSS).
  • The promoter is the stretch of DNA just before the TSS where the switches that control the gene sit. In this project we use the window from 1000 letters before to 500 letters after each TSS.
  • An E-box is one such switch: the sequence CACGTG that CLOCK:BMAL1 binds. If a variant changes those letters, the switch may stop working.

2 · Chronomic medicine: the clock meets genetic disease

Our group studies what happens when the clock meets rare genetic disease — chronomic medicine. A variant that changes when a disease gene is switched on, rather than the protein it makes, could change how severe the disease is, or be missed entirely because it sits outside the protein-coding part of the gene. The group has already found that variants in E-boxes of the heart gene SCN5A may matter in the arrhythmia disorder Brugada syndrome (Ampiah and Hearn 2025; Seed and Hearn 2021; Malik and Hearn 2020; McGowen et al. 2025; Seed and Hearn 2022). This project asks the same kind of question, but in the brain.

3 · Why epilepsy and brain channelopathy genes?

Seizures are famously tied to the clock and to sleep: many epilepsies are worse at particular times of day or after sleep loss (Ruben et al. 2018; Jones et al. 2019; Malik and Hearn 2020; Khan et al. 2018). The genes behind many of these conditions are ion channels and transporters — so-called channelopathies. If the clock helps time these genes, their promoters should carry E-boxes, and the DNA letters of those E-boxes should be protected from change. That is what you will test.

You will work with two gene lists, both from Genomics England PanelApp (Martin et al. 2019):

  • Brain channelopathy — 21 well-established ("green") genes, including SCN1A, SCN8A, CACNA1A, KCNQ2 and SLC2A1. This is your close-focus set.
  • Early-onset or syndromic epilepsy — 632 green genes. This larger set gives you the numbers to test ideas statistically.

4 · Your hypothesis and your question

Hypothesis

If CLOCK:BMAL1 timing matters for these genes, then E-boxes in their promoters will be (a) present more often than chance, (b) unusually conserved across mammals, and (c) carry fewer and rarer human variants than comparable positions — the signature of purifying selection removing changes that break the switch.

Concretely, you will answer:

  1. Where are the E-boxes in these promoters, and are there more than you would expect by chance?
  2. Which human variants (from the 1000 Genomes Project) break or make an E-box, and how common are they in different populations?
  3. Are E-box positions more conserved, and less variable, than matched control positions — as selection would predict?
  4. Which variants are the best candidates to follow up in the 100,000 Genomes Project epilepsy patients?

5 · What you will produce

  • A catalogue of E-box-changing variants in these promoters, with population frequencies.
  • A figure and a statistic for the conservation/selection test.
  • A ranked shortlist of candidate variants, and a short written report that someone else could reproduce.

Every one of those is a real result, whichever way the numbers come out. A clean "no signal" answer, properly shown, is a finding too.

Promoters and coordinates

An E-box is only a circadian switch if it sits in the right place — the promoter. Here you learn how genomic positions are written down, and how to pull a promoter's DNA out of the reference genome.

The promoter window

For every gene we defined the promoter as the window from 1000 letters before the TSS to 500 after — 1500 letters in all, measured along the direction the gene is read. Those windows are saved as a BED file, the standard way to write "a stretch of a chromosome":

head /data/examples/genes/promoters.bed
Reading a BED line

chr2 166127019 166128519 SCN1A 0 - means chromosome 2, from position 166,127,019 to 166,128,519, for gene SCN1A, on the - strand. BED counts from 0 and the end is "one past the last base" (so this is 1500 bases). Many bioinformatics mistakes are off-by-one errors from mixing this up with 1-based counting — watch for it.

Pull out a promoter's sequence

We also saved the DNA of every promoter as a FASTA file, promoters.fa, one record per gene. samtools faidx fetches one by name:

samtools faidx /data/examples/reference/promoters.fa SCN1A

The header line records where the sequence came from — the chromosome, the genomic start and end, and the strand. You will need that genomic start later to turn a position within the promoter into a position on the chromosome.

How GC-rich are these promoters?

Promoters near a TSS are often rich in C and G. That matters for E-boxes, because CACGTG is itself C/G-heavy. Measure it with seqtk:

seqtk comp /data/examples/reference/promoters.fa | awk '{gc+=$4+$5; tot+=$2} END{printf "overall GC = %.1f%%\n", 100*gc/tot}'

Keep that number in mind: when you ask whether E-boxes are "more common than chance", chance has to mean chance given this GC content, not a flat 25% per letter.

Checkpoint

There are 641 promoters (the 21 channelopathy and 632 epilepsy genes, de-duplicated, with one mitochondrial gene dropped), each 1500 bases long, and the overall GC content is about 59%. If your numbers match, your reference and your coordinates line up and you are ready to hunt for E-boxes.

Find the E-boxes

The canonical clock E-box is six letters: CACGTG. Your first real task is to find every one of them in the promoters, and write down exactly where each sits on the genome.

One useful fact: CACGTG reads the same both ways

CACGTG is a palindrome — its reverse complement is itself (C↔G, A↔T, read backwards). DNA has two strands, but because this motif is a palindrome you only have to search one of them and you have found it on both. That simplifies the code.

Scan every promoter

Open a Python notebook. This reads the promoter FASTA, finds every CACGTG, and turns each hit's position within the promoter into a position on the chromosome using the genomic start stored in the FASTA header:

import re

def read_fasta(path):
    name, seq, out = None, [], []
    for line in open(path):
        if line.startswith(">"):
            if name: out.append((name, "".join(seq)))
            name, seq = line[1:].strip(), []
        else:
            seq.append(line.strip().upper())
    if name: out.append((name, "".join(seq)))
    return out

records = read_fasta("/data/examples/reference/promoters.fa")

eboxes = []
for header, seq in records:
    gene = header.split()[0]
    region = header.split()[1]            # e.g. chr2:166127020-166128519
    chrom = region.split(":")[0]
    gstart = int(region.split(":")[1].split("-")[0])   # 1-based genomic start of the window
    for m in re.finditer("(?=CACGTG)", seq):           # (?=...) finds overlapping hits too
        off = m.start()
        eboxes.append((chrom, gstart + off - 1, gstart + off + 5, gene))  # BED: 0-based start

print("canonical E-boxes found:", len(eboxes))
eboxes[:5]

Flexible motifs: position weight matrices

Proteins do not demand a perfect sequence. CLOCK:BMAL1 binds CACGTG best, but it also binds close relatives. To find those, you use a position weight matrix (PWM) — the standard tool for describing what a protein likes to bind.

What a PWM is

A PWM comes from many real binding sites. For each position it records how often each letter appeared. We give you the experimentally measured matrix for BMAL1 (called Arntl) from the JASPAR database (Castro-Mondragon et al. 2022):

cat /data/examples/motif/ARNTL_MA0603.2.jaspar

Read down the columns: the matrix is 8 letters long, and its favourite letter at each position spells GTCACGTG — the E-box CACGTG with a little flank. A PWM lets a site score well even if it is not a perfect CACGTG.

Turn counts into scores, and scan

This builds a log-odds score (how much more a letter is liked than background), scores every window on both strands, and reports a relative score from 0 to 1 (1 = the matrix's perfect site). Run it in your Python notebook:

import math
# --- read the JASPAR count matrix ---
counts = {}
for line in open("/data/examples/motif/ARNTL_MA0603.2.jaspar"):
    if line.startswith(">"): continue
    base = line[0]
    counts[base] = [int(x) for x in line[line.index("[")+1:line.index("]")].split()]
L = len(counts["A"])
bg = {"A":0.295,"C":0.205,"G":0.205,"T":0.295}      # rough promoter background

# log-odds weight for each base at each position (with a small pseudocount)
w = {}
for i in range(L):
    tot = sum(counts[b][i] for b in "ACGT") + 4*0.8
    for b in "ACGT":
        w[(b,i)] = math.log2(((counts[b][i]+0.8)/tot)/bg[b])
best = sum(max(w[(b,i)] for b in "ACGT") for i in range(L))
worst = sum(min(w[(b,i)] for b in "ACGT") for i in range(L))

def relscore(s):                       # 0..1 relative score of an 8-mer
    if any(c not in "ACGT" for c in s): return -1
    sc = sum(w[(c,i)] for i,c in enumerate(s))
    return (sc - worst) / (best - worst)

comp = str.maketrans("ACGT","TGCA")
def revcomp(s): return s.translate(comp)[::-1]
def best_both(s): return max(relscore(s), relscore(revcomp(s)))

Now scan every promoter and keep windows scoring at least 0.85 (a common, fairly strict cut-off). Reuse the read_fasta and records from the previous step:

THR = 0.85
pwm_sites = []
for header, seq in records:
    gene = header.split()[0]
    chrom = header.split()[1].split(":")[0]
    gstart = int(header.split()[1].split(":")[1].split("-")[0])
    i = 0
    while i <= len(seq) - L:
        sc = best_both(seq[i:i+L])
        if sc >= THR:
            pwm_sites.append((chrom, gstart+i-1, gstart+i+L-1, gene, round(sc,3)))
            i += L            # don't double-count the same site
        else:
            i += 1
print("PWM sites (score >= 0.85):", len(pwm_sites))

import pandas as pd
chan = set(pd.read_csv("/data/examples/genes/brain_channelopathy.tsv", sep="\t")["gene"])
print("channelopathy genes with >=1 PWM site:",
      len({g for *_, g, _ in [(c,s,e,g,sc) for c,s,e,g,sc in pwm_sites] if g in chan}))

At score ≥ 0.85 you should find about 1240 sites, and this time about 28 of them fall in 13 of the 21 channelopathy genes — including the ones the exact search missed. Think about the threshold

Lower the cut-off to 0.80 and you get many more sites; raise it to 0.90 and you get far fewer. There is no single "correct" value — it trades false positives against false negatives. When you report a result, you must state the threshold you used. Try two values and see how much your later conclusions depend on it.

Are there more E-boxes than chance?

Finding 205 E-boxes means nothing until you know how many you would expect by chance. Getting "chance" right is the whole game here — and it is where a careful scientist earns their result.

The trap: CACGTG hides a CpG

Look at the middle of CACGTG: it contains the dinucleotide CG (a "CpG"). CpGs are special in two ways — they are rarer in the genome than you'd expect, and they mutate faster than other sites. So a naive expectation ("four letters, each 1-in-4, so CACGTG happens 1 in 4⁶") is wrong twice over. You must compare against a background that already knows this promoter's letter and dinucleotide composition.

A fair expectation

Fit a simple model to the promoters themselves — how often each letter follows each other letter — then ask how many CACGTG it predicts. Run in Python (reuse records):

from collections import Counter
pool = "".join(seq for _, seq in records)
base = Counter(c for c in pool if c in "ACGT"); N = sum(base.values())
di = Counter(pool[i:i+2] for i in range(len(pool)-1) if pool[i] in "ACGT" and pool[i+1] in "ACGT")

def P(a): return base[a]/N
def T(a,b):                                   # P(next=b | this=a)
    s = sum(di[a+x] for x in "ACGT"); return di[a+b]/s if s else 0.25

word = "CACGTG"
p_word = P(word[0])
for i in range(len(word)-1): p_word *= T(word[i], word[i+1])

Allele frequency, and why populations matter

Allele frequency is the fraction of chromosomes in a group that carry the alternative letter. AF=0.5 means half; AF=0.0003 means it was seen on just a couple of chromosomes. A variant can be common in one population and absent in another — which is why a single global number can mislead, and why the file keeps each super-population separately.

Scoring the damage a variant does

"Inside an E-box" is yes/no. Better is how much a variant changes the switch. Score the sequence with the reference letter, score it again with the variant's letter, and take the difference. This is exactly how published tools (like motifbreakR) work — and you can do it in a few lines.

The idea

For a variant, slide the BMAL1 matrix across the DNA around it. In each window, compute the relative score with the reference letter and with the alternative letter. The window where the motif is strongest is the one that matters; the change in score (alt − ref) tells you whether the variant weakens the switch (negative), strengthens it, or does nothing.

Compute it

This reuses w, relscore, best_both and L from the PWM step, and var from the variants step. First index the promoter sequences by gene:

gene_seq = {}
for header, seq in records:
    gene  = header.split()[0]
    chrom = header.split()[1].split(":")[0]
    gstart= int(header.split()[1].split(":")[1].split("-")[0])
    gene_seq[gene] = (chrom, gstart, seq)

def motif_effect(gene, pos, ref, alt):
    chrom, gstart, seq = gene_seq[gene]
    off = pos - gstart                       # 0-based index into the promoter sequence
    if off < 0 or off >= len(seq) or seq[off] != ref:
        return None                          # variant not in this window / reference mismatch
    best = None
    for wst in range(max(0, off-L+1), min(off, len(seq)-L)+1):
        ref_w = seq[wst:wst+L]
        alt_w = ref_w[:off-wst] + alt + ref_w[off-wst+1:]
        rr, ra = best_both(ref_w), best_both(alt_w)
        if best is None or max(rr,ra) > max(best[0],best[1]):
            best = (rr, ra)
    rr, ra = best
    return round(rr,3), round(ra,3), round(ra-rr,3)

Conservation: has evolution kept this letter still?

If an E-box really is an important switch, breaking it should have been bad for our ancestors too — so evolution should have kept those letters unchanged across species. A conservation score measures exactly that.

What phyloP is

phyloP compares the same position across many species' genomes and asks whether it has changed less (conserved — positive score) or more (fast-evolving — negative score) than neutral DNA (Pollard et al. 2010; Christmas et al. 2023). We use the 470-way mammalian alignment from the Zoonomia project, the state of the art for spotting constrained regulatory DNA (Pollard et al. 2010; Christmas et al. 2023). A score above ~2 means a position has been held unusually still across mammals.

We saved phyloP for every base of every promoter so you don't have to download the huge genome-wide file:

The selection test

This is the heart of the project. If E-boxes are functional switches kept intact by selection, then compared with fair control positions they should be more conserved and carry fewer, rarer variants. Here you measure both — carefully.

Choosing a fair control

You cannot compare E-boxes with "all promoter DNA", because CACGTG contains a CpG, and CpGs behave differently from ordinary DNA (they mutate faster and are themselves often conserved near promoters). The fair comparison is E-box positions versus other CpG positions in the same promoters. Then any difference you see is about the E-box, not about CpGs in general.

Your shortlist of candidates

You now have, for every promoter variant: whether it hits an E-box, how much it changes the motif score, how conserved the position is, and how common it is. Combine these into a ranked shortlist — the variants worth taking into the patient data.

What makes a strong candidate

  • It really disrupts the switch — in a canonical E-box, or a large negative PWM score change.
  • The position is conserved — high phyloP, so evolution cares about it.
  • It is in a relevant gene — a channelopathy or epilepsy gene, ideally your close-focus 21.
  • Its frequency is informative — rare variants fit a "damaging" story; a common one that still disrupts a conserved switch is a puzzle worth explaining.

  • Tandem E-boxes. The strongest circadian switches are often two E-boxes about 6–7 bases apart. Search your E-box list for pairs with that spacing — are the tandem ones even more conserved?
  • Does function predict constraint? Plot PWM score against phyloP for every E-box. If high-scoring sites are the most conserved, that is strong evidence they are real.
  • Other non-canonical E-boxes. You focused on CACGTG and the PWM. Try the near-relatives CACGTT and CATGTG — enriched? conserved?
  • Expression timing. Cross your genes against a database of rhythmically expressed human genes (Ruben et al.) — do the genes with conserved E-boxes tend to be the rhythmic ones?
  • A different gene set. The whole pipeline is a function of the gene list. Swap in a cardiac panel (closer to the group's SCN5A work) or a neurodevelopmental one and compare.
  • gnomAD. Check your top variants in gnomAD v4 (800k genomes) for a much finer frequency and any constraint signal.
The transferable point

Every extension is the same shape: define regions, find a motif, overlay variation, test against a control. You now know that shape — it is a large part of regulatory genomics. The specific question (clocks in brain genes) is new; the method is yours to reuse.

Using the AI assistant

The AI tab is optional. Used well, it is a patient tutor that never tires of your questions. Used badly, it writes code you don't understand and sometimes invent facts. Here is how to get the good version.

What it is good for

  • Explaining an error. Paste the red error message and ask what it means.
  • Explaining a line of code in this guide you don't follow.
  • Translating between Python and R, or suggesting a plot.
  • Unsticking you when you know what you want but not the command.

The rules (again, because they matter)

  1. No personal or patient data in the AI tab. This project is public data only — fine.
  2. You own the result. If the AI gives you a number or a conclusion, do not report it until you have produced it yourself from the data and can point to the command.
  3. Read every line it writes and run it yourself. If you can't explain what a line does, ask it to explain, or don't use it.
  4. A key is a password. You connect your own model with an API key; it stays in this browser tab only. Don't share it.
A good habit

When the AI writes code, ask it a follow-up: "what would break this?" or "what am I assuming here?". The goal of this project is that you can do regulatory genomics — the assistant is there to help you learn it, not to do it instead of you.

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the words this project uses. Search the guide (top left) to jump to where each one is used.

Allele frequency (AF)
The fraction of chromosomes in a group that carry a particular variant letter. Low = rare, high = common.
BED file
A plain-text list of genomic regions (chromosome, start, end). Counts from 0; the end is one past the last base.
BMAL1 / CLOCK
The two proteins that join and switch on clock-controlled genes by binding E-boxes.
Channelopathy
A disease caused by a faulty ion channel — many epilepsies are channelopathies.
Conservation
How little a DNA position has changed across species. Measured here by phyloP; high = constrained.
CpG
A C followed by a G. CpGs mutate faster than other DNA and behave specially — which is why they need a fair control.
E-box
The DNA switch CLOCK:BMAL1 binds. The canonical one is the palindrome CACGTG.
Enrichment
Finding something more often than a fair "by chance" expectation would predict.
FASTA
A text format for DNA/protein sequence: a > header line, then the letters.
GRCh38
The current human reference genome build. All coordinates here are GRCh38.
phyloP
A per-position conservation score from comparing many species; positive = conserved, negative = fast-changing.
Promoter
The DNA just before a gene's start where its switches (including E-boxes) sit.
Purifying selection
Evolution removing harmful changes, leaving important positions conserved and depleted of variation.
PWM (position weight matrix)
A flexible description of what sequence a protein binds, scored from 0 to 1, that allows near-misses.
SNV
A single-nucleotide variant: one DNA letter changed.
TSS
Transcription start site: where a gene begins to be copied into RNA.
VCF
The standard file format for variants: position, reference letter, alternative letter, and information fields.
