Clock E-boxes in brain genes

In this project you will look at whether common human genetic variants disrupt the DNA sequences the body clock uses to time brain genes, and whether those sequences appear to be protected by evolution. Everything runs in this web page: a terminal, Python and R notebooks, gene databases and a genome browser. You don't need to install anything.

The question in brief

The clock proteins CLOCK and BMAL1 work as a pair, switching genes on by binding a short DNA sequence called an E-box (CACGTG). In the promoters of genes that cause epilepsy and brain “channelopathies”, how often do human variants destroy or create an E-box? Are those variants rare or common? And do E-boxes sit at positions that have changed unusually little during evolution?

The research question

The background you need, and the question you will answer. Come back to this page whenever you lose track of why a step matters.

1 · The circadian clock

Nearly every cell in the body has its own circadian clock. At its centre are two proteins, CLOCK and BMAL1, which pair up and switch other genes on. They do this by binding a short DNA sequence near the start of a gene, called an E-box. The strongest circadian E-box is the six-base palindrome CACGTG. Genes with an E-box in their promoter can be turned up and down over the 24-hour day, and a large fraction of the genome is controlled in this way (Takahashi 2017; Partch et al. 2014; Koike et al. 2012; Mohawk et al. 2012).

Promoter, TSS and E-box
  • A gene is transcribed (copied into RNA) starting at its transcription start site (TSS).
  • The promoter is the stretch of DNA around the TSS that holds most of the sequences controlling the gene. In this project we use the region from 1000 bases before each TSS to 500 bases after it.
  • An E-box is one of those control sequences: CACGTG, the site CLOCK:BMAL1 binds. A variant that changes any of its six bases may stop it working.

2 · Chronomic medicine

Our group works on what happens where the clock meets rare genetic disease, which we call chronomic medicine. A variant that changes when a disease gene is switched on, rather than the protein it makes, could make the disease milder or more severe. It could also be missed altogether, because it lies outside the protein-coding part of the gene. We have already found that variants in E-boxes of the heart gene SCN5A may matter in the arrhythmia disorder Brugada syndrome (Ampiah and Hearn 2025; Seed and Hearn 2021; Malik and Hearn 2020; McGowen et al. 2025; Seed and Hearn 2022). This project asks the same kind of question about genes that matter in the brain.

3 · Why epilepsy and brain channelopathy genes?

Seizures are closely linked to sleep and the clock: many epilepsies are worse at particular times of day or after a night of poor sleep (Ruben et al. 2018; Jones et al. 2019; Malik and Hearn 2020; Khan et al. 2018). Many of the genes behind these conditions encode ion channels and transporters, and the diseases they cause are known as channelopathies. If the clock helps to control when these genes are active, their promoters should contain E-boxes, and the bases in those E-boxes should be protected from change. That is what you will test.

You will work with two gene lists, both from Genomics England PanelApp (Martin et al. 2019):

  • Brain channelopathy: 21 well-established ("green") genes, including SCN1A, SCN8A, CACNA1A, KCNQ2 and SLC2A1. These are the genes you will look at most closely.
  • Early-onset or syndromic epilepsy: 632 green genes. This larger set gives you enough data for statistical tests.

4 · Hypothesis and questions

Hypothesis

If timing by CLOCK:BMAL1 matters for these genes, the E-boxes in their promoters should (a) occur more often than expected by chance, (b) be unusually well conserved across mammals, and (c) carry fewer, and rarer, human variants than comparable positions. The last two together would be the signature of purifying selection, which removes changes that damage the site.

In practice, you will answer four questions:

  1. Where are the E-boxes in these promoters, and are there more than you would expect by chance?
  2. Which human variants (from the 1000 Genomes Project) destroy or create an E-box, and how common are they in different populations?
  3. Are E-box positions more conserved, and less variable, than matched control positions, as selection would predict?
  4. Which variants are the best candidates to follow up in the epilepsy patients of the 100,000 Genomes Project?

5 · What you will produce

  • A catalogue of the variants that change E-boxes in these promoters, with their frequencies in different populations.
  • A figure and a statistical test for conservation and selection.
  • A ranked shortlist of candidate variants, and a short written report that would let someone else reproduce your results.

Each of these is a result whichever way the numbers come out. If you find no signal, showing that clearly is still a finding.

Promoters and coordinates

An E-box can only act as a circadian switch if it is in the right place, which is the promoter. This page shows how positions in the genome are written down, and how to pull a promoter's DNA sequence out of the reference genome.

The promoter window

For every gene we defined the promoter as the window from 1000 bases before the TSS to 500 bases after it, measured in the direction the gene is read. That is 1501 bases in total: the 1000 before, the TSS itself and the 500 after. The windows are saved as a BED file, the standard format for describing stretches of a chromosome:

head /data/examples/genes/promoters.bed
Reading a BED line

chr2 166127519 166129020 SCN1A 0 - is the line for SCN1A, which is on chromosome 2, on the - strand. BED coordinates start from 0, and the end position is one past the last base. So in the usual 1-based counting this window runs from base 166,127,520 to base 166,129,020, and its length is simply end − start = 1501 bases. Mixing up the two ways of counting causes a lot of off-by-one errors in bioinformatics, so watch out for it. (Because SCN1A is on the - strand and read from right to left, the 1000 bases "before" its TSS at 166,128,020 are the ones to the right of it.)

Pull out a promoter's sequence

We also saved the DNA sequence of every promoter in a FASTA file, promoters.fa, with one record per gene. samtools faidx fetches a record by name:

samtools faidx /data/examples/reference/promoters.fa SCN1A

samtools faidx labels its output with the name only (>SCN1A). The header line in the file itself says more about where the sequence came from: the chromosome, the genomic start and end (1-based), the strand and the TSS. You can see it with grep:

grep "^>SCN1A " /data/examples/reference/promoters.fa

You will need the genomic start later, to convert a position within the promoter into a position on the chromosome. (The sequence is always given on the forward strand of the chromosome, reading left to right, whichever strand the gene is on.)

How GC-rich are these promoters?

Promoters near a TSS are often rich in C and G. That matters for E-boxes, because CACGTG itself is mostly C and G. Measure it with seqtk:

seqtk comp /data/examples/reference/promoters.fa | awk '{gc+=$4+$5; tot+=$2} END{printf "overall GC = %.1f%%\n", 100*gc/tot}'

Keep this number in mind. When you ask whether E-boxes are more common than chance, "chance" has to take this GC content into account; you can't assume that each base turns up 25% of the time.

Checkpoint

There are 641 promoters (the 21 channelopathy and 632 epilepsy genes, with duplicates removed and one mitochondrial gene dropped), each 1501 bases long, and the overall GC content is about 59%. If your numbers match, your reference and coordinates are consistent and you are ready to look for E-boxes.

Find the E-boxes

The canonical clock E-box is the six bases CACGTG. The first task is to find every copy of it in the promoters and record where each one is on the genome.

CACGTG reads the same on both strands

CACGTG is a palindrome: its reverse complement (swap C with G and A with T, then read it backwards) is the same sequence. DNA has two strands, but because the motif is palindromic, searching one strand finds every copy on both. That keeps the code simple.

Scan every promoter

In a Python notebook, run the code below. It reads the promoter FASTA file, finds every CACGTG, and converts the position of each hit within the promoter into a position on the chromosome, using the genomic start stored in the FASTA header:

import re

def read_fasta(path):
    name, seq, out = None, [], []
    for line in open(path):
        if line.startswith(">"):
            if name: out.append((name, "".join(seq)))
            name, seq = line[1:].strip(), []
        else:
            seq.append(line.strip().upper())
    if name: out.append((name, "".join(seq)))
    return out

records = read_fasta("/data/examples/reference/promoters.fa")

eboxes = []
for header, seq in records:
    gene = header.split()[0]
    region = header.split()[1]            # e.g. chr2:166127520-166129020
    chrom = region.split(":")[0]
    gstart = int(region.split(":")[1].split("-")[0])   # 1-based genomic start of the window
    for m in re.finditer("(?=CACGTG)", seq):           # (?=...) finds overlapping hits too
        off = m.start()
        eboxes.append((chrom, gstart + off - 1, gstart + off + 5, gene))  # BED: 0-based start

print("canonical E-boxes found:", len(eboxes))
eboxes[:5]

Flexible motifs: position weight matrices

Proteins don't need a perfect sequence to bind. CLOCK:BMAL1 binds CACGTG best, but it also binds sequences that differ from it slightly. To find those, you use a position weight matrix (PWM), the standard way of describing which sequences a protein binds.

What a PWM is

A PWM is built from many known binding sites. For each position in the site, it records how often each base was found there. We have given you the experimentally measured matrix for BMAL1 (listed as Arntl) from the JASPAR database (Castro-Mondragon et al. 2022):

cat /data/examples/motif/ARNTL_MA0603.2.jaspar

Read down the columns. The matrix is 8 bases long, and the most common base at each position spells GTCACGTG: the E-box CACGTG with two extra bases in front. With a PWM, a site can score well even if it isn't a perfect CACGTG.

From counts to scores

The code below turns the counts into log-odds scores (how much more often each base appears in binding sites than in background DNA), scores every 8-base window on both strands, and gives each window a relative score from 0 to 1, where 1 is the best possible match to the matrix. Run it in your Python notebook:

import math
# --- read the JASPAR count matrix ---
counts = {}
for line in open("/data/examples/motif/ARNTL_MA0603.2.jaspar"):
    if line.startswith(">"): continue
    base = line[0]
    counts[base] = [int(x) for x in line[line.index("[")+1:line.index("]")].split()]
L = len(counts["A"])
bg = {"A":0.295,"C":0.205,"G":0.205,"T":0.295}      # rough promoter background

# log-odds weight for each base at each position (with a small pseudocount)
w = {}
for i in range(L):
    tot = sum(counts[b][i] for b in "ACGT") + 4*0.8
    for b in "ACGT":
        w[(b,i)] = math.log2(((counts[b][i]+0.8)/tot)/bg[b])
best = sum(max(w[(b,i)] for b in "ACGT") for i in range(L))
worst = sum(min(w[(b,i)] for b in "ACGT") for i in range(L))

def relscore(s):                       # 0..1 relative score of an 8-mer
    if any(c not in "ACGT" for c in s): return -1
    sc = sum(w[(c,i)] for i,c in enumerate(s))
    return (sc - worst) / (best - worst)

comp = str.maketrans("ACGT","TGCA")
def revcomp(s): return s.translate(comp)[::-1]
def best_both(s): return max(relscore(s), relscore(revcomp(s)))

Now scan every promoter and keep the windows that score at least 0.85, a commonly used and fairly strict cut-off. This reuses read_fasta and records from the previous step:

THR = 0.85
pwm_sites = []
for header, seq in records:
    gene = header.split()[0]
    chrom = header.split()[1].split(":")[0]
    gstart = int(header.split()[1].split(":")[1].split("-")[0])
    i = 0
    while i <= len(seq) - L:
        sc = best_both(seq[i:i+L])
        if sc >= THR:
            pwm_sites.append((chrom, gstart+i-1, gstart+i+L-1, gene, round(sc,3)))
            i += L            # don't double-count the same site
        else:
            i += 1
print("PWM sites (score >= 0.85):", len(pwm_sites))

import pandas as pd
chan = set(pd.read_csv("/data/examples/genes/brain_channelopathy.tsv", sep="\t")["gene"])
chan_sites = [site for site in pwm_sites if site[3] in chan]      # site = (chrom, start, end, gene, score)
print("PWM sites in the channelopathy genes:", len(chan_sites),
      "in", len({site[3] for site in chan_sites}), "of the 21 genes")
Checkpoint

With a cut-off of 0.85 you should find about 1240 sites. This time about 28 of them are in 13 of the 21 channelopathy genes, so the flexible search finds sites in genes that the exact search missed. (The numbers can shift slightly if you change how the scan moves along the sequence or the cut-off, so record which you used.) Save these sites as a BED file too, as you did for the exact matches.

Choosing a cut-off

Lower the cut-off to 0.80 and you will get many more sites; raise it to 0.90 and you will get far fewer. There is no single correct value: a lower cut-off finds more true sites, but more false ones as well. Whatever you choose, state it when you report a result. Try two values and see how much your later conclusions depend on the choice.

Are there more E-boxes than chance?

Finding 205 E-boxes tells you little until you know how many you would expect by chance. Working out that expectation properly is the most important part of this step.

CACGTG contains a CpG

The middle of CACGTG is the dinucleotide CG, known as a CpG. CpGs behave differently from other DNA: they are rarer in the genome than you would expect, and they mutate faster than other sites. The simple expectation (four bases, each with a one in four chance, so CACGTG should turn up once in every 4⁶ positions) takes no account of either. You need to compare against a background that reflects these promoters' base composition and their dinucleotide composition.

A fair expectation

Fit a simple model to the promoters themselves, which records how often each base is followed by each other base, and ask how many copies of CACGTG it predicts. Run this in Python (it reuses records):

from collections import Counter
pool = "".join(seq for _, seq in records)
base = Counter(c for c in pool if c in "ACGT"); N = sum(base.values())
di = Counter(pool[i:i+2] for i in range(len(pool)-1) if pool[i] in "ACGT" and pool[i+1] in "ACGT")

def P(a): return base[a]/N
def T(a,b):                                   # P(next=b | this=a)
    s = sum(di[a+x] for x in "ACGT"); return di[a+b]/s if s else 0.25

word = "CACGTG"
p_word = P(word[0])
for i in range(len(word)-1): p_word *= T(word[i], word[i+1])
positions = sum(max(0, len(seq)-5) for _, seq in records)
expected = p_word * positions
observed = len(eboxes)                         # from the find step
print(f"observed {observed} | expected ~{expected:.0f} | ratio {observed/expected:.2f}")
Checkpoint

You should see about 205 observed against ~123 expected, a ratio of about 1.66. (Try the same calculation for CACGTT, a related sequence that CLOCK:BMAL1 binds only weakly. Is it enriched?)

Why the baseline matters

With the simple 1 in 4⁶ expectation you would have reported a much larger enrichment, and a reviewer would rightly have rejected it, because CpG content alone could explain it. A fair baseline is what separates a finding from an artefact. Write down in your notes which baseline you used and why.

How much does a variant change the motif?

Whether a variant is inside an E-box is a yes-or-no question. It is more useful to know how much it changes the site. Score the sequence with the reference base, score it again with the variant's base, and take the difference. Published tools such as motifbreakR work in the same way, and you can do it in a few lines of code.

How it works

For each variant, slide the BMAL1 matrix along the DNA around it. In each window, work out the relative score with the reference base and with the alternative base. The window where the motif scores highest is the one that matters, and the change in score (alt − ref) tells you whether the variant weakens the site (a negative change), strengthens it, or makes no difference.

The code

This reuses w, relscore, best_both and L from the PWM step, and var from the variants step. First, index the promoter sequences by gene:

gene_seq = {}
for header, seq in records:
    gene  = header.split()[0]
    chrom = header.split()[1].split(":")[0]
    gstart= int(header.split()[1].split(":")[1].split("-")[0])
    gene_seq[gene] = (chrom, gstart, seq)

def motif_effect(gene, pos, ref, alt):
    chrom, gstart, seq = gene_seq[gene]
    off = pos - gstart                       # 0-based index into the promoter sequence
    if off < 0 or off >= len(seq) or seq[off] != ref:
        return None                          # variant not in this window / reference mismatch
    best = None
    for wst in range(max(0, off-L+1), min(off, len(seq)-L)+1):
        ref_w = seq[wst:wst+L]
        alt_w = ref_w[:off-wst] + alt + ref_w[off-wst+1:]
        rr, ra = best_both(ref_w), best_both(alt_w)
        if best is None or max(rr,ra) > max(best[0],best[1]):
            best = (rr, ra)
    rr, ra = best
    return round(rr,3), round(ra,3), round(ra-rr,3)

Then apply it to every variant and classify each one, using the same 0.85 cut-off as before:

THR = 0.85
rows = []

    eff = motif_effect(r.gene, r.pos, r.ref, r.alt)
    if not eff: continue

    else: cls="none"

        rows.append((r.gene, r.chrom, r.pos, r.ref, r.alt, r.AF, rr, ra, d, cls))

Conservation

If an E-box is an important control site, damaging it would have been harmful to our ancestors as well, so evolution should have kept its sequence the same across species. A conservation score measures this.

What phyloP is

phyloP compares the same position across the genomes of many species and asks whether it has changed less than neutral DNA (it is conserved, and gets a positive score) or more (it is evolving quickly, and gets a negative score) (Pollard et al. 2010; Christmas et al. 2023). We use the 470-way mammalian alignment from the Zoonomia project, currently one of the best resources for finding constrained regulatory DNA (Pollard et al. 2010; Christmas et al. 2023). A score above about 2 means a position has changed unusually little across mammals.

We saved the phyloP score for every base of every promoter, so you don't need to download the very large genome-wide file:

The PWM score from Part 3 is a prediction based on a matrix measured in the lab. phyloP is independent evidence from evolution: it knows nothing about CLOCK:BMAL1, only which positions have stayed the same over millions of years. If a predicted site is also highly conserved, the case that it is a working site is much stronger.

The selection test

This is the central test of the project. If E-boxes are working control sites that selection keeps intact, then compared with fair control positions they should be more conserved and carry fewer, rarer variants. On this page you measure both.

Choosing a fair control

You can't simply compare E-boxes with all promoter DNA, because CACGTG contains a CpG, and CpGs behave differently from ordinary DNA: they mutate faster, and near promoters they are often conserved themselves. The fair comparison is between E-box positions and other CpG positions in the same promoters. Any difference you then see is down to the E-box, not to CpGs in general.

Your shortlist of candidates

For every promoter variant you now know whether it is in an E-box, how much it changes the motif score, how conserved its position is and how common it is. Combine these into a ranked shortlist of the variants most worth following up in patient data.

What makes a strong candidate

  • It clearly disrupts the site: it is in a canonical E-box, or it causes a large drop in PWM score.
  • The position is conserved: a high phyloP score suggests evolution has kept it the same.
  • It is in a relevant gene: a channelopathy or epilepsy gene, ideally one of your 21 channelopathy genes.
  • Its frequency tells you something: rare variants fit the idea that disrupting the site is harmful, while a common variant that disrupts a conserved site is a puzzle worth explaining.

Writing it up

Your write-up should let someone else, or you in six months' time, reproduce every number in it. This page suggests a structure for the report and lists what to hand in.

Keep notes as you go

Don't leave this to the end. After each step, add a short Markdown cell to your notebook saying what you did, which file and command you used, and the number you got. Together, these notes become your methods section. Recording how every result was produced is one of the most valued habits in computational biology, and reproducibility is a particular interest of our group.

Structure of the report

IntroductionThe clock, E-boxes, and why epilepsy and channelopathy genes. One paragraph, citing papers from the reading list.
MethodsThe gene panels (PanelApp, with versions), how promoters were defined (TSS −1000/+500), how E-boxes were defined (exact CACGTG and PWM ≥ 0.85, naming the matrix), the variant data (1000 Genomes 30×), the conservation scores (phyloP 470-way) and your control (CpG-matched). State every threshold.
ResultsEnrichment (observed against expected), the size of the catalogue, the selection test (figure and statistic) and the shortlist. Give each number alongside what it is being compared with.
DiscussionWhat the results mean, the confounding factors you noted, and what a follow-up in the 100,000 Genomes Project would test.

Limitations

  • A PWM match and conservation suggest that a site is functional; they don't prove that CLOCK:BMAL1 binds it in neurons.
  • Part of the conservation signal may come from position within the promoter, close to the TSS (you controlled for CpGs, but not for everything).
  • You used reference populations from the 1000 Genomes Project, not patients, so the frequencies are population frequencies, not evidence of disease association.
  • Your results depend on the thresholds you chose (the PWM cut-off and the promoter window). If they still hold at a second threshold, say so.

  • Your output files (eboxes_canonical.bed, catalogue.csv, positions.csv and your figure), downloaded together as a .zip file from My files (top right).
  • Your written report.

Where you could take it

If the main project goes well and you want to do more, here are some possible extensions. Each one uses skills you already have.

  • Tandem E-boxes. The strongest circadian control sites are often two E-boxes about 6–7 bases apart. Search your E-box list for pairs with that spacing. Are the tandem ones even more conserved?
  • Does site strength predict constraint? Plot PWM score against phyloP for every E-box. If the highest-scoring sites are also the most conserved, that is good evidence that they are functional.
  • Other non-canonical E-boxes. You concentrated on CACGTG and the PWM. Try the related sequences CACGTT and CATGTG. Are they enriched, or conserved?
  • Timing of expression. Compare your genes with a database of rhythmically expressed human genes (Ruben et al.). Do the genes with conserved E-boxes tend to be the rhythmic ones?
  • A different gene set. The whole analysis starts from the gene list, so you can swap in a cardiac panel (closer to the group's work on SCN5A) or a neurodevelopmental one and compare.
  • gnomAD. Look up your top variants in gnomAD v4 (over 800,000 people, about 76,000 of them with whole genomes) for much more precise frequencies and any sign of constraint.
The general method

All of these extensions follow the same pattern: define regions, find a motif, overlay variation and test against a control. That pattern covers a large part of regulatory genomics. The question here, about the clock in brain genes, is new, but the method works for many others.

Using the AI assistant

The AI tab is optional. It can be a useful tutor, explaining an error or a piece of code as many times as you need. But it can also write code you don't understand, and it sometimes makes things up, so use it with care.

What it is good for

  • Explaining an error. Paste in the red error message and ask what it means.
  • Explaining a line of code in this guide that you don't follow.
  • Translating between Python and R, or suggesting a plot.
  • Getting unstuck when you know what you want to do but not which command does it.

The rules

  1. Never put personal or patient data into the AI tab. This project uses only public data, so that shouldn't come up.
  2. You are responsible for the result. If the AI gives you a number or a conclusion, don't report it until you have produced it yourself from the data and can point to the command you used.
  3. Read every line of code it writes, and run it yourself. If you can't explain what a line does, ask it to explain, or don't use it.
  4. Treat your API key like a password. You connect your own AI model with an API key, which is kept only in this browser tab. Don't share it.
A good habit

When the AI writes code for you, ask a follow-up question such as "what could make this go wrong?" or "what am I assuming here?". The aim of this project is for you to learn to do regulatory genomics. The assistant is there to help you learn, not to do the work for you.

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms used in this project. You can search the guide (top left) to find where each one comes up.

Allele frequency (AF)
The fraction of chromosomes in a group that carry a particular variant. Low means rare and high means common.
BED file
A plain-text list of genomic regions (chromosome, start, end). Positions are counted from 0, and the end is one past the last base.
BMAL1 / CLOCK
The two proteins that pair up and switch on clock-controlled genes by binding E-boxes.
Channelopathy
A disease caused by a faulty ion channel. Many epilepsies are channelopathies.
Conservation
How little a DNA position has changed across species. Measured here with phyloP; a high score means the position is constrained.
CpG
A C followed by a G. CpGs mutate faster than other DNA and behave differently in other ways too, which is why comparisons need a CpG-matched control.
E-box
The DNA sequence that CLOCK:BMAL1 binds. The canonical E-box is the palindrome CACGTG.
Enrichment
Finding something more often than a fair estimate of chance would predict.
FASTA
A text format for DNA or protein sequences: a > header line, followed by the sequence.
GRCh38
The current human reference genome build. All the coordinates in this project are on GRCh38.
phyloP
A conservation score for each position, from comparing many species. Positive means conserved; negative means changing quickly.
Promoter
The stretch of DNA at the start of a gene that holds the sequences controlling it, including E-boxes.
Purifying selection
Evolution removing harmful changes, so that important positions stay conserved and carry little variation.
PWM (position weight matrix)
A flexible description of the sequences a protein binds, which allows near-matches as well as perfect ones. Here, sites are scored from 0 to 1.
SNV
A single-nucleotide variant: a change to one base of DNA.
TSS
Transcription start site: where a gene starts to be copied into RNA.
VCF
The standard file format for variants: position, reference base, alternative base and information fields.
